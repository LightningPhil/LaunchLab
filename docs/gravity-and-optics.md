# Gravity and optics lessons

Implemented 19 October 2026. The orrery and the three wiki lessons run entirely from packaged code and data. Sources below were checked on that date. The explanations are original teaching material.

## Lagrange points in the orrery

A point belongs to a **pair**, rather than to a body alone. The default-off **Lagrange points** control opens a pair selector and **Fit points**. Sun–planet pairs cover Mercury through Neptune and Pluto. Earth–Moon and Jupiter–Ganymede cover the remaining launch worlds; the Sun is the primary in the solar pairs. Pluto–Charon is included to show a real pair whose triangular points are unstable in the circular model. **All launch worlds** displays all 60 points. Selecting a launch world in the Explore menu selects its pair when a focused pair is being shown.

The shared model, `src/lagrange-model.ts`, uses the circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP). With companion mass fraction μ, the barycentric positions of the primary and companion are (−μ, 0) and (1−μ, 0). Separation, total GM and angular speed are each one. A pair orbit takes 2π time units. We solve the rotating acceleration equation separately in each of its three continuous collinear intervals by bisection. This avoids applying the small-mass Hill approximation to Pluto–Charon. L4/L5 are the exact equilateral coordinates (½−μ, ±√3/2).

L1–L3 are unstable. L4/L5 are marked linearly stable only when 27μ(1−μ) < 1. This is a statement about sufficiently small disturbances in this ideal model; it does not imply damping, capture, or stability after an arbitrary kick.

`src/orrery-lagrange.ts` places this construction in the actual orbital plane. Its radial direction is the current primary-to-companion vector. Relative positions at ±60 seconds supply the velocity direction; the orbital normal and tangent determine which triangle leads (L4) and trails (L5), including inclined moon planes. At **True scale**, coordinates are scaled by the instantaneous pair separation. At **Even spacing**, the already illustrated pair positions set the span. Close L1/L2 offsets are enlarged enough to clear the companion's drawn radius or complete visible moon-system footprint, bounded by clearance from the primary. The slider blends these offsets continuously. The point card always reports the physical circular-model distance, rather than an illustrated screen distance.

These are instantaneous, oriented **circular-model guides**, not exact equilibrium locations in the real eccentric, perturbed Solar System. The existing approximate ephemerides, including the Earth–Moon barycentre represented as Earth and the simplified Pluto system, retain their documented limits. The overlay does not integrate or alter any world’s orbit. Small diamonds and captions use the existing depth and partial-silhouette masks. The diamonds remain visible when captions are turned off. No collision-avoidance layout is added to the labels. The camera's maximum zoom is extended to 200,000× so Fit points can frame physical moon pairs as well as illustrated lanes; Home restores the whole-system view.

## Data and independent checks

Body GM values are in km³/s². Planetary and lunar values use the body entries in the [JPL/NAIF DE440 mass-parameter kernel](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/pck/gm_de440.tpc), rather than the larger planet-plus-satellite-system entries. Ganymede uses JUP365 and Charon PLU043 values linked through [JPL satellite physical parameters](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/phys_par/). The nominal astronomical unit is 149,597,870.7 km, as listed among [JPL astrodynamic parameters](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/astro_par.html). Mean planetary separations follow the existing orrery elements; moon separations are the mean values used by its moon model. Radii are rounded mean radii used only for probe contact and schematic body drawings.

The checked-in `tests/fixtures/lagrange-jpl.json` records independent barycentric equilibrium coordinates from the [JPL Three-Body Periodic Orbits API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/periodic_orbits.html), with exact query URLs and returned system metadata for Earth–Moon, Sun–Earth and Sun–Mars. Each test solves using **that catalogue's own mass ratio**, then compares all five coordinates to a tolerance of 2×10⁻¹¹ separation units (under 3 m at 1 AU). JPL's rounded catalogue coordinates do not support machine-epsilon comparisons. Its Sun–Earth catalogue ratio also differs from our body-only Sun–Earth ratio: the catalogue uses an effective Earth–Moon mass. The fixture is a check of the equations and coordinate convention, not a claim of metre-level real-world prediction.

Further references: [ESA’s Lagrange-point explanation](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Operations/What_are_Lagrange_points), the [CR3BP equations in Orbital Mechanics & Astrodynamics](https://orbital-mechanics.space/the-n-body-problem/circular-restricted-three-body-problem.html), and [NASA’s explanation of Webb’s orbit near L2](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/orbit/). Webb follows a controlled orbit around the vicinity; it does not rest at a place with zero gravity.

## Interactive Lagrange experiment

Students choose a pair, L1–L5, an offset to the right, an upward velocity perturbation, and a duration. Advanced controls allow fictional mass ratios from 1:1 to 10,000,000:1 while retaining the selected pair's distance and radii. Custom experiments use pair-orbit time units rather than claiming a physical period for those invented masses.

The planar rotating equations include gravity, centrifugal acceleration, and the Coriolis terms +2vy and −2vx. Error-controlled DOP853 (order 8) replaces the original fixed-accuracy RK4 scheme. The production run uses relative tolerance 10⁻¹³ and absolute tolerance 10⁻¹⁵ on all four state components; a second run uses 2.3×10⁻¹⁴ and 2×10⁻¹⁶ with a smaller maximum step. Steps also respect local gravity and travel timescales. Runs stop at a densely located model surface, four pair separations, the selected duration, or a numerical agreement limit. They have bounded iteration counts. Jacobi drift and the largest sampled position disagreement between the runs are exposed in model details. Conservation alone is not treated as a proof of trajectory accuracy. See [the numerical audit and verification](lagrange-accuracy.md).

The offset is divided by 100 exactly once to convert the control's percentage to separation units: Earth–Moon 0.1% means 384.4 km. Initial rotating-frame velocity is (0, kick/100). Zero kick therefore means corotation at release, rather than zero inertial velocity. The plotted departure is the Euclidean distance from the equilibrium, including the initial offset; it is not distance travelled since release or numerical integration error.

The illustrated flight view offers three cameras. **Watch the orbit** converts each recorded position to an inertial frame using its own sample time: (x cos t − y sin t, x sin t + y cos t). **Ride with the pair** retains the rotating coordinates, keeping the worlds and equilibria fixed. **Close-up on point** subtracts the selected equilibrium and converts both displacement axes to kilometres using the pair separation, with identical pixels per kilometre horizontally and vertically. These are coordinate changes of the same solution, not different simulations. Balance points are selectable with pointer or keyboard, and the space view shows a probe, enlarged world artwork, the barycentre and the latest orbit of its trail. Frame bounds stay fixed during playback.

The **departure-versus-time** graph measures 100 times the distance from the selected equilibrium, in pair-separation units. Current and pinned runs share axes; each is measured from its own starting point. The faint curve is the full computed prediction and the bright curve is the replay so far. A largest-predicted-departure reading provides immediate feedback while adjusting settings. Changing the pair starts at time zero, clears the comparison, resets close-up scale and restores real masses. Point, nudge, kick, duration and camera are retained. Other parameter changes preserve the inspected fraction of the run, with zero-duration surface contacts handled explicitly. Preset missions compare Earth–Moon L4, Earth–Moon L1 and Pluto–Charon L4; a preset that changes pair also clears the comparison. Only matching pairs and masses share a pinned flight trail on the space view.

The close-up keeps the exact equilibrium at a gold crosshair and shows the release marker, complete replay trail, faint full prediction and an optional comparison measured from its own equilibrium. Actual world radii are used if a world enters the field. **Magnify launch** rewinds and sets a horizontal half-width of six initial offsets (at least 0.001 separations); zoom and **Fit full drift** set a fixed field. An edge arrow and kilometre readout identify an off-screen probe without implying escape. Slow close-up replay, on by default, takes twenty viewing seconds per pair orbit; the other cameras compress the whole run into twelve seconds. All use the same stored trajectory and replay slider.

The accompanying explanation distinguishes unstable equilibria from linearly stable triangular points. An unstable point can amplify small errors in position or velocity; real spacecraft use orbit determination and planned thruster corrections (station keeping) to maintain a desired nearby trajectory. The ideal equilibrium stays fixed in the rotating frame: it is the probe that drifts relative to it. A linearly stable point can support bounded oscillations without damping, while real disturbances and mission requirements may still require corrections. See [NASA’s Webb orbit explanation](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/orbit/) and [NASA’s explanation of mid-course corrections and station keeping](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/webb/2021/12/27/more-than-you-wanted-to-know-about-webbs-mid-course-corrections/).

Pinning records the parameters and the part of the run already inspected. Playback, scrubbing, probe markers and distance readouts evaluate the same seventh-order continuous solution, rather than linearly joining sparse output states. Drawing vertices follow the adaptive integration mesh and include dense midpoints and refined radial-distance maxima, so close approaches and the largest predicted departure are resolved. Run brings the space view and its transport controls into view together. Reduced-motion visitors can inspect the completed run without animated playback. Navigation saves a paused session; hidden or off-screen tools pause their animation. Field labels and values occupy separate, fixed-height rows, so changing number or unit lengths cannot reflow the controls. Contextual help supports hover, keyboard focus, tappable question marks and Escape dismissal.

## Escape velocity experiment

`src/wiki/escape-model.ts` uses the launch simulator's existing `ENVIRONMENTS`: GM = gR². This deliberate consistency differs slightly from the precision GM data used for pair mass ratios. At starting radius r = R + h, the threshold is √(2GM/r), circular speed is √(GM/r), and specific energy is v²/2 − GM/r. Probe mass changes energies in joules, not either speed threshold. Energy conservation supplies the entire outward coast and its turning point or asymptotic speed. The comparison graph uses a shared vertical speed scale and distance measured in each run's starting-radius units; changing the world does not pretend the same radius fraction is the same distance in kilometres.

The graph includes **infinity**, using the compact distance coordinate u = 1 − r₀/r. Tick marks 1, 2, 5, 10 and ∞ explicitly identify the compressed, non-uniform distance scale. Conservation of energy gives v² = v₀² − vesc² u. A bound coast ends at u = (v₀/vesc)² < 1 with zero speed; exactly at escape speed the curve approaches zero at u = 1; faster coasts retain their asymptotic speed at that edge. End markers and an outcome card distinguish these cases, including returning flights whose turning points lie far beyond the former six-radius window. The threshold curve and pinned coast use the same mapping and speed axis. A starting-speed gauge compares the chosen speed with the threshold.

The starting-speed slider spans zero to **twice the escape speed at the current launch height**, with escape at its midpoint. Changing worlds retains the thumb’s fraction of this range and recalculates km/s for the new world; height and probe mass retain their positions. Changing height retains physical speed unless it exceeds the new upper limit, when it is clamped. This lets students test the effect of altitude on a fixed initial speed. Three presets select 80%, 100% or 120% of the current threshold. A pinned experiment retains its complete physical settings. This is an instantaneous radial push followed by an unpowered coast around an isolated spherical world. Atmosphere, rotation, other bodies and continued thrust are omitted. Gas giants and the Sun retain Launch Lab's fictional reference platform. Escaping one world does not automatically escape its parent or the Sun.

Reference: [OpenStax University Physics, gravitational potential energy and escape speed](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/13-3-gravitational-potential-energy-and-total-energy).

## Telescope experiment

Reflectors and mirror-based telescopes are the same family. The lesson compares a lens refractor with a Newtonian reflector: the latter's curved primary focuses light and a flat diagonal folds the beam towards the eyepiece. The ray drawings are schematic, with compressed dimensions; the numerical readings use the entered parameters.

Students change aperture D, objective and eyepiece focal lengths, star separation, and (under advanced controls) wavelength λ and reflector secondary diameter fraction ε. Magnification is |fobjective/feyepiece|, focal ratio fobjective/D, exit pupil D/magnification, and unobstructed collecting area πD²/4. A central obstruction multiplies the area by 1−ε². The displayed 1.22λ/D Rayleigh angle is explicitly an **unobstructed reference**, including when a reflector is selected.

The plot adds intensities of two equal, incoherent point stars, using ideal Fraunhofer diffraction. A circular pupil has amplitude 2J1(x)/x. An annular pupil has amplitude 2[J1(x)−εJ1(εx)]/[x(1−ε²)], where x = πD sinθ/λ and the small-angle approximation is used at arcsecond scales. Intensity is squared amplitude; each star's peak is normalized to one. Collection is reported separately, so normalized peaks do not imply equal total brightness. J1 uses a convergent series near the origin and an asymptotic expansion beyond 16. The angular plot range remains ±8 arcseconds for current and pinned instruments. Changing an eyepiece cannot change these angular diffraction curves.

Assumptions: perfect monochromatic optics, no seeing, coating loss, mirror supports, optical aberrations or eye response. A wider exit pupil than the eye can accept would waste some light; the tool reports the optical pupil rather than guessing the observer's pupil. Refractor colour correction and practical reflector limitations are discussed in the readings without falsely simulating them.

References: [OpenStax Astronomy, telescopes](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy/pages/6-1-telescopes), [University Physics, telescope magnification](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-8-microscopes-and-telescopes), [circular apertures and resolution](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/4-5-circular-apertures-and-resolution), [NASA's telescope teaching guide](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/ngsttelescope-guide.pdf), and [NIST DLMF §10.17](https://dlmf.nist.gov/10.17) for the Bessel asymptotic expansion.

## Verification

`tests/science-labs.test.mjs` checks launch-world coverage, root ordering and rotating-force residuals over small and comparable masses, independent JPL equilibria, the triangular stability transition, finite date/slider endpoints, True-scale invariance and leading/trailing orientation. Probe checks cover Jacobi conservation, step convergence, the difference between disturbed L1 and L4, body contact and bounded execution. Escape tests check known speeds, mass independence, energy conservation and altitude. Optics tests check scaling, obstruction area, Bessel reference values, the Airy zero and symmetric incoherent profiles. Wiki tests cover the new topics, search and every authored equation.

Browser checks cover the default-off orrery control, focused and all-pairs selection, point details and the lesson link, the complete distance slider, More bodies, caption visibility, and narrow-screen controls. All three lesson tools are exercised with comparisons; changing telescope eyepiece alone leaves the plotted angular profile unchanged. Phone layouts use larger SVG type and stacked controls. The expanded orrery toolbar's measured height keeps its information card below the controls.

The revised wiki navigation gives every interactive lesson an original SVG illustration, coloured card, short invitation and Interactive lab badge, matching the cannon and rocket entries. Additional checks cover the compressed plot's finite and infinite endpoints, distance preservation under the inertial-frame rotation, fixed layout geometry through slider extrema, retained replay position, keyboard selection, contextual help and the compact layout.
